# Chapter 4. The Mechanic

<!-- c04.s01 -->

At the top of the world the water was black and perfectly still, and everything in it glowed.

He stepped off the Dragon onto a shelf of smooth dark rock, and it went on past him, rib after rib, at the pace of a man walking. Then its light was dwindling round the far curve of the Well, and he could see where he was.

He stood on the lip of a hole in the world. The shelf ran away to either side of him in a great curve, and before him the rock simply ended, and there was nothing. It was not darkness as he had known it in the Tangle. It was depth. The water was so clear and so still that he could feel the whole height of it under him, and his stomach turned over. Far, far down, at the bottom of that black shaft, there was a single white star.

"The Lantern," said Okonta. "The hospital. You'll see it close, if he lets you."

Lantern jellies drifted over the gulf. Brask had called them lights, and that was what they were, great soft bells of pale blue fire the size of houses, trailing veils that glimmered and went out and glimmered again. Nothing moved fast. Nothing made a sound. He had come from a forest that never stopped singing to a place that seemed to be holding its breath.

Okonta's passengers were met at the landing. Two healers in grey had come up from the Well for the old man from Fallow, as though they had known he was coming. They had a litter with them, slung between two lantern jellies on cords. The daughter went with it. She looked back at Wrack once from the edge. Then they sank away into the black, the two blue lights growing small, toward the star.

The house stood a little way along the shelf, where the rock broke the surface.

It was half in the water and half out of it, under a sky the colour of a pigeon's breast, with a low red sun rolling along the rim of the world. There was a line of washing, quite stiff with frost. At one end was a workshop with its door standing open on a clutter of half-mended things. A kettle sat on the step. There were no guards and no gates.

It was empty.

"Sometimes they're south," Okonta said, and shrugged. "They've a house at the other end as well, just like it. They go to and fro as they please, and nobody knows which they're in. The rays know. The rays don't tell me." She sat down on the step beside the kettle. "We wait. It's his rule: anyone may wait, nobody goes in the workshop."

They waited eleven days.

Brask came on the second, as he had said, with two cousins as big as himself and a sledge of smoked cod. He stacked it on the step without being asked and stayed to drink something hot out of the kettle. After that somebody from Rime came every other day. They were blunt, loud, generous people, who laughed at Wrack's thin southern tunic and brought him a sealskin coat. They would not hear of payment. A girl of ten showed him how to walk on the ice above the house. He stood up there in the long red twilight with the cold burning his face. The white went on to the edge of the sky. He understood that he was at the end of the world.

Mostly he sat on the lip of the Well and practised asking.

Okonta could not teach him; she had no gift. But she had kept company with callers for thirty years, and she knew how they began. "Don't ask her to do anything," she said. "Ask her what she's touching. That's the first thing a child learns. Callers name it Listening."

So he sat with his hand in the water and asked, as politely as he knew how, and for three days nothing happened. On the fourth he felt the shelf. He felt it not under his hand but all of it, the whole curve, as though his hand had grown. He felt the weed on it, and a crab walking. The next day he felt the jellies, each a slow cold pulse. The day after that he reached down into the Well as far as he dared. Something very large was coiled against the wall a kilometre below him, and it had felt him too. He took his hand out of the water quickly.

His eyes went often to the workshop door. His hands wanted to go in. He did not let them.

On the twelfth day the Dragon came round again. An old man came up the path from the landing with a basket of crabs. He was arguing mildly with the woman beside him about whether they had left a window open at the other house.

He was small. He was older than anything Wrack had ever seen, older than the lamp in Low Anthem, with skin like paper that has been folded many times. He walked like a man whose feet hurt. He glanced at Okonta, nodded, glanced at Wrack, and put the basket down.

"Somebody's been in my Dragon," Jef said.

"I'm sorry. It was—"

"Did I say I minded? The third ventral coupling. You reseated it backwards."

"It runs cooler that way."

There was a pause.

"Yes," Jef said slowly. "It does. I've been meaning to try that for two hundred years." He turned to the woman. "Maelis. He reseated it backwards."

The Queen of the Stingrays was tall and straight and grey-haired, and she wore a patched coat with the pockets full of something. Behind her, out in the dark water of the Well, shapes the size of ships were turning slowly. Wrack felt himself looked at by minds a great deal older than the house.

"The rays have been talking about you for a month," she said. "They say the sea has been shouting. Come in. You'll want soup."

Okonta had said he would be disappointed, and he was, for about an hour.

The inside of Kettle House was one long room with a stove at one end and a table that had been mended many times. There were shelves of jars and a box bed behind a curtain. A lantern jelly no bigger than a fist pulsed in a glass bowl on the windowsill; Maelis fed it crumbs. Jef took off his boots with a groan and put his feet on the stove rail. He complained about the crabs, the damp, and the state of his knees. He asked Okonta whether the Rime people had been leaving fish again and whether anybody had thought to bring up the washing. He was a tired old man in his own kitchen. There was no god in the room anywhere.

Maelis cooked. She split the crabs with a short knife and put them in a pot with weed from the garden. When she set a bowl in front of Wrack she looked at him properly for the first time. Her gaze went over his face as Ione's intended water had gone through his skull.

"Somebody tied your hands," she said.

"Yes."

"And you don't know who."

"I don't know anything before the kelp."

"Mm." She gave him a spoon. "Eat. He'll want to look at your hands and he'll be rude about it. He's rude when he's frightened."

"I am not frightened," Jef said, from the stove. "I'm nine hundred years old. I've been frightened of everything already." But when the bowls were empty he pushed his own aside and said, "Well. Let's see them."

He took Wrack's hands in his and turned them over under the lamp. He looked at the fingertips, and pressed the pads. He bent the thumbs back.

"No calluses to speak of. Never held a hook before last month. A pen, though. You've a pen-bump, look, there." He let go. "Okonta says you opened a hall lamp that I'd have said was dead for good. And then my Dragon. Who taught you?"

"Nobody. My hands knew."

"Hands don't know anything. Heads know, and tell the hands, and forget they did it." He leaned back. He was studying Wrack now with a fixed bright attention that was not rude at all. "I learned from walls. I'll tell you about that some time. It took me eleven years to open my first seam, and I had the pictures in front of me. And you just reach up." He shook his head. "Go to bed. There's a loft. In the morning you'll come into the shop and I'll put something in front of you and we'll see what your head's forgotten."

In the loft, under a sealskin, Wrack lay and listened to them talking below, the old man's grumble and her low replies. Once Jef laughed, a short surprised bark. Outside the window the jellies went by. He slept better than he had since the kelp.

By day Jef taught him the craft.

The workshop was the largest room in the house and the only untidy one. There were lamps in pieces, and things like the lamp's heart laid open, and coils of a pale wire that was warm to the touch. Along one wall hung tools. They were small, and of no shape Wrack could have described, and each had been worn by a hand until it shone.

"The mariners say tend, mend, make," Jef said, on the first morning. He was hunting for something under a bench. "Tend is what the holds do. Wipe it, sing to it, don't poke it. I've nothing against that. It's kept the machines alive three thousand years, which is more than poking would have. Mend is what I do. Find out why it's stopped, and stop it stopping. Make." He came up with a dented grey ovoid and blew dust off it. "Make is building a new one from nothing. I can do that too. Slowly. One air-heart takes me four years. There are two thousand air-hearts in the world, and they're all the same age." He set the thing on the bench. "This is a warm-stone. It heats a room. It's the simplest thing they made. Tell me what's wrong with it."

It was not like learning. It was like being reminded.

Jef would open a machine on the bench and begin to explain it, and Wrack's hands would already be moving. The old man watched this, day after day, with an expression that was hard to read. He was a good teacher, and it was plain that he had expected to be needed more than he was. He was impatient with words, and endlessly patient with the work itself. He would show a thing once and then go and sit on a stool with a mug while Wrack did it wrong six times. On the seventh he would say, "There," as if he had known all along which try it would be.

"You've done this before," he said once. "Not *this*. But something. You've stood over drawings. You've told other people where to put things. You think in sections, look, you always start by working out where you'd cut it." He sniffed. "Agravar training. All those straight lines."

"Is that bad?"

"It's different. The Makers didn't think in straight lines. They thought like water. Everything they built wants to flow from somewhere to somewhere, and when it stops, it's silted up, or it's seated wrong, or it's worn through. That's all there is. Clear it, seat it, or replace it." He drank. "You'll be better than me at seeing where to cut. And worse at knowing when not to. Hand me that."

In the afternoons, when Jef's hands tired, they sat on the step, and it was then that the old man talked. He did not tell his story. He let pieces of it fall, like a man emptying his pockets.

He had been a sick child. That was how he had come to the Lantern. He had had a wasting fever at six years old, and had been carried north on the Dragon by an aunt, and left in a pool in the fourth hall. The healers cured him in a season. Nobody came to fetch him. "There was a war on, down in the Anthem Waters," he said. "People had other things to think of." So he had stayed. He swept the halls and carried for the healers. And at night, when the patients slept, a small boy with nothing to do wandered through nine halls of Maker glass, and in those days the walls still spoke.

"Archive walls," Jef said. "You've not seen one working. Hardly anyone alive has. You'd put your hand on the glass and it would light, and there'd be pictures. Moving. How a thing was built. Every part, one after another, as slow as you liked." He held his palm up flat, remembering it. "The healers thought it was pretty. I thought it was the only thing in the world. I sat in front of those walls for eleven years. I learned their writing off them. I'd get a tool out of a store nobody'd opened in two thousand years and hold it up to the picture to see which way round it went."

"Where are the walls now?"

"Dark. They went one at a time. The last one in the Lantern went out when I was about three hundred. I've never found out how to mend them; they never showed me themselves." He turned the mug in his hands. "So it's all in here now." He tapped his forehead. "One old head. I've told the elders for six centuries that it's a stupid place to keep it."

Of the war, and of Sounding, he said nothing at all. Wrack did not ask.

In the second week Jef took him down the Well.

They went on a deep-runner, which was a needle's stout cousin, blunt and thick-hulled and slow. Jef rode it as Okonta rode hers, without touching anything. The descent took nine hours. The water grew cold, and then colder, and so heavy that Wrack's bones ached and each breath was like lifting a weight. The jellies were left behind. The green weed gave out. There was nothing but the runner's small light on the black wall of the trench, going by, and the star below them, which did not seem to grow.

Halfway down something uncoiled from the wall.

It was the thing he had felt. An eye the size of a cartwheel regarded them, gold, with a slotted pupil. Behind the first there were others, a whole wall of them, stirring.

"Well-wardens," said Jef. "Squid. Don't stare, it's rude." He held up a small worn disc of the pale metal, and the arm withdrew. "Nobody put them here. They came on their own, about the time the hospital did, and they've let nobody down since who meant harm. I've no idea how they tell. I asked Maelis to ask the rays, and the rays thought it was funny."

Then at last the star grew, and came apart into nine.

The Lantern stood on the floor of the Well. It was nine great halls of Maker glass, set in a ring and joined by covered ways, each hall as large as the whole dome of Low Anthem. The glass was the colour of milk and lit from within, so that the hospital stood in the black water like a crown of lamps. And all around it, even here, even in that cold, things grew: white fans, and pale flowers the size of his hand, feeding on the warmth that leaked from the walls.

Inside it was warm and bright and very quiet. In the pools the sick of the world floated, in water that glowed faintly gold. Healers sat at the edge of every pool with their hands in the water. They worked in shifts, Jef said, three to a pool, day and night, so that the intention never lapsed. Wrack saw a child whose hand was growing back; it was translucent still, like a hand made of wax, and she was holding it up to the light and turning it. He saw a man lying with his chest open like a book, and under the water things were being put right in it. He saw an old woman, brought from the far side of the world, whose lungs had been remade. She was breathing in her sleep without effort. Two pools along, in the same hall, the old man from Fallow lay with his daughter's hand on his forehead.

The gift was strong here. He could feel the sea leaning close, as a person leans over a cradle.

"Why here?" he asked. "Why is it stronger?"

"Don't know. I'm a mechanic." Jef was walking slowly, nodding to healers, who bowed to him in a way he clearly disliked. "Maelis says it's because it's the oldest water there is. It went down at the Tips before there were people and it's never been anywhere else. Nothing's ever been put in it but care." He stopped at a tall archway. "Here's what I wanted you to see."

The hall beyond was dark. The pools were dry. So was the hall after it, and the two after that. Four of the nine.

"Five hundred years I've been patching this place," Jef said. He did not grumble it. He said it quietly, with his hand on the cold glass of the arch. "There's one great heart under the middle of the ring that feeds all nine halls. It's going. I can't get at it without shutting down the five that still work, and if I shut them down, the water in those pools goes dead in an hour, and so do some of the people in them. So I've mended round the edges. And every hundred years or so, another hall goes." He looked at Wrack sidelong. "Unless somebody could hold the water in five halls just as it is. Warm, and still, and cared for. While somebody else had his hands in the works."

"I can't intend. I don't know how."

"You don't need to. The healers will go on intending. You only need to keep the water from knowing the heart's stopped." He scratched his chin. "It's a fool's job. I'd not ask if I had another two hundred years."

It took Wrack six days.

He sat in the centre of the ring with his hands in a channel of seawater, and asked the sea, pool by pool, to stay as it was. That was Holding. He had done it at Kettle House with a cupful. Here there were three hundred pools, and a person in each. He did not shout. He was too frightened to shout. He asked, and went on asking, hour after hour, and felt the Well lean in around him and take the weight. Under the floor Jef worked, with a lamp between his teeth, calling up for tools. On the fourth day Wrack went down to help him, still holding, and discovered that he could do both. One part of him kept three hundred pools still, and another lay on its back in a crawlway beside a nine-hundred-year-old man, handing him things.

On the seventh day all nine halls came alight together.

The healers stood in the covered ways and wept. A very old one, a woman who had been at the Lantern sixty years, went into the fourth hall and stood in the middle of it, turning round and round. Jef said "Hm," and went to see about lunch.

By night the sea taught him the rest. Jef could not help with that.

"I've no gift," the old man said. He was sitting on the step with a cup of something hot, watching. It had become his habit. "Never had. I'm a mechanic. The sea doesn't talk to me; I just fix her plumbing. The callers have the gift, and they can't tell a coupling from a clam. That's how it's always been. Makers' craft in one skin and sea's gift in another, and never the two together." He drank. "Then you."

It was Maelis who taught him the names of what he was doing. She was a healer, not a caller, but she had sat with callers at the end of their strength for fifty years and knew the road. Listening came first, she said, and he had found that for himself. After it came Moving, the plain push and pull, a current where there had been none. Then Shaping, giving water an edge and a form, a wall, a rope, a bubble of air. Then Holding, which he had learned at the Lantern in the hardest way. Raising came last of all, the lifting of water against its own weight. Most callers lived and died without it.

"You began at the wrong end," she said. "You raised a ship before you could listen to a crab. It's as if a child ran before it had seen its own feet. No wonder she knocked you down." She was sitting beside Jef with a rug over her knees. "Go back to the beginning. Do each one small. Ask for less than you think you can have. You'll be astonished how much she gives a person who doesn't grab."

So he went back. Night after night he sat at the lip of the Well, under the turning sun, and asked for small things. He asked for a ripple, and then for a current to carry one jelly a little to the left. He made a rope of water and tied it in a knot. He held a bowl of sea upside down in the air until it was easy, and then until it was dull. Every night the sea gave him a little more, as though it had only been waiting for manners. He was ebbed twice, mildly, a day of cold and thirst each time. After the second he began to know the feeling of the edge before he reached it.

At the end of a month a sphere of water a hundred metres across hung in the air over the Well, turning slowly, with a living shoal inside it. The low sun shone through it and laid a moving red light across the ice.

"Why me?" he said. He set it down as gently as an egg.

"Don't know," said Jef. "Maelis thinks you do, somewhere under all that."

"I think you had a foot in each world before you had a name," Maelis said. "I think that's what the sea likes about you. But I'm guessing. The rays know, and they won't say. They say it's yours to find."

She went in. Jef stayed, turning the cup in his hands.

"I'll tell you what I do know," he said. "I'm dying. Don't make that face. I've had nine hundred years and I'm sick of my own cooking. I found a thing in the walls once, when I was young, that keeps a body going. Maker medicine. It's a machine, like everything of theirs. I've mended it as often as it'll stand." He rubbed his chest absently. "When I go, there's nobody. The lamps go, one at a time. The air-hearts. The holds, and the Dragon. No more Threaded, because I'm the only one who can lay the Thread. A couple of million people in the dark, slowly. It might take two hundred years. That's the worst of it. It'd be so slow that nobody would ever decide to do anything."

"You could teach someone."

"I've tried. Sixty apprentices in nine centuries. Clever ones. They could learn to tend. A handful could mend a little, with me standing over them. Not one could carry it away. One carried off everything but the hands, and I'd as soon not talk about him. I used to think it was the mariner in them. Too patient. Too respectful of the thing." He nodded at the place in the sky where the sphere had hung. "I was looking for a mechanic. I'd have settled for a middling one. I did not expect to get *that*."

He went in after a while. Through the window Wrack saw him stop behind Maelis's chair and rest his hand on her shoulder. She put her own up to cover it without looking round.

He came to know her slowly. She did not let fall pieces of herself as Jef did. She answered what she was asked, exactly, and then stopped.

She had been born at the other end of the world, in the Quiet, and trained as a healer at the Hush, the southern hospital. At twenty she had been sent to gather venom. The great rays lived in those waters, and their sting killed in seconds, and a drop of it rightly used could wake a stopped heart. The healers took it from the spines the rays shed. She had gone to the place where they gathered, and one of them had come and looked at her.

"That's all," she said. "It looked at me. For about a day. When it had finished I knew things I hadn't known, and I could tell what it wanted. They don't speak. They remember, and they can put a memory into you as you'd put a cup in someone's hand. They wanted somebody who could say it in words to the rest of us, now and then. I was standing there." She was stripping weed for the pot. "The mariners called me their queen. It's a silly word. I'm their mouth."

"What are they?"

"Older than the Makers. Older than the holds. They've watched everything that ever happened in the sea, and they've kept it." She glanced at the window, where the long shapes turned. "They keep what the undertow takes, too. Did Ione tell you that? No, she wouldn't know. Nothing the sea is given is ever lost. It goes to them."

He set down the knife he was holding.

"Then they have it. What I lost."

"Yes."

"Can they give it back?"

"Through me, they can. There's a thing I make." She went on with the weed. "I'm not going to make it yet. You're not ready to want it, and he hasn't finished with you. And there's a question I ask first. I ask everyone. You've not got an answer to it."

"How did you come to marry him?"

She smiled then, for the first time. It changed her whole face.

"He came south to mend the warm-stones at the Hush, fifty-one years ago. He was eight hundred and something. I was twenty-four and I'd been the rays' mouth for four years, and everyone I met either knelt to me or ran away. He asked me to hold a lamp for him. Then he told me I was holding it wrong." She shook her head. "There were wives before me. Seven. He remembers every one of them, and he's never once called me by the wrong name. I asked the rays if it would be a foolish thing to do. They showed me him at twenty. So I did it."

"What was he like at twenty?"

Maelis did not answer for some time.

"Sorry," she said. "He was very, very sorry. Pass me the pot."

The visitor came in the seventh week, on a day of thin fog.

Okonta knew first. She had been mending a strap on the step, and she stopped with the awl in the air and turned her head toward the south.

"Needle," she said. "One. Coming up under the ice." She frowned. "I know every hull in the north by its voice. I don't know that one. It's an old pattern. I've seen its like in a cradle on the Dragon, with nobody to ride it."

Out in the Well, the rays had stopped turning. Wrack had never seen them still. They hung in the black water all facing one way, like weathervanes.

Maelis came to the door with a cloth in her hands. She looked at the rays, and her face shut. It was like a house with the shutters put up.

"Put the kettle on," Jef said, to nobody. He had come out of the workshop. He stood wiping his fingers, slowly, one at a time. "No. I'll do it. Wrack, fetch another bowl."

It came up at the landing without a sound. It was a needle, shorter than Okonta's and grey with age, and its flank was scored as if it had been dragged over stone. The rider got off it awkwardly. He wore a hard suit. It was surface work, the finest Wrack had seen, dark and close-fitting, with a helmet of clear glass. He waded ashore, and undid the helmet, and breathed the cold air with evident relief.

He was an old man. He was not old as Jef was; he was perhaps seventy. It was the face of a man who has been listened to for a long time. Above the collar of the suit, thin pale scars ran down from his temples and along his neck.

Okonta saw them. Wrack heard the breath go out of her.

"Hello, old man," the visitor said.

"Anselm," said Jef. "You've got fat."

"I've got rich. It comes to the same thing at my age." He looked past Jef to the door. "Maelis."

"You'll not come into my kitchen," Maelis said. Her voice was quite steady. "You can have the step. It's his rule that anyone may wait. It was never mine." She went in and shut the door, and they heard the bar go across.

So the three of them sat on the step in the fog, with soup. Okonta stood a little way off with her arms folded, and did not sit at all. The visitor ate neatly. He praised the crab. He asked after Jef's knees. He might have been a nephew on a duty call. Only his eyes kept coming back to Wrack. They had gone to him in the first moment, and rested there, as a man's eyes rest on something of his own that he has come upon in a neighbour's yard. He had not seemed surprised.

"And this is the one," he said at last. "The long-call is full of him. A stranger's hands."

"He can open a seam," Jef said.

"So I had always understood." The visitor set down his bowl. "I'll say what I came to say, and then I'll go. I've found your city. The one under the Girdle. You told me about it when I was twenty-three. You'd looked for it for a hundred years, you said, and given it up. It's there. It's whole. It lit from end to end, once, for one man, and went dark again when he left it." He did not look at Wrack as he said it. "There's an engine in the lowest vault. My people have had the half of it out, like a tooth, and bolted it to a frame. It runs about as well as a tooth would. I want it seated as it was meant to be, and I've nobody now who can. Come south and do it. Or tell me how."

"What does it do?"

"You know what it does."

"I know what sort of thing they'd have put in a lowest vault," Jef said. "I'd not seat it for you if you carried me there on your back."

"No. I thought not." The visitor nodded, as at a column of figures that has come out as he expected. "You'll have told him about the holds, I suppose. That they're going. You told me, on this step. I was twenty-two, and I didn't sleep for a month. You've had your hand cupped round a candle for nine hundred years, old man, and you call it a life's work. Up there are forty million people on a few rocks. I can give them ground. Real ground, to stand on, that nobody is standing on already."

"At whose cost?"

"At the cost of people who are dead already and haven't been told."

"Go home, Anselm."

"Yes." He stood, and picked up the helmet. Then he turned to Wrack, directly, for the first time. His voice was gentle. "You don't know your name, do you?"

Wrack's mouth had gone dry. "No."

"I do. I could tell it to you now, in a breath. I could tell you what you were, and what you were paid, and what you're worth. It's a great deal." He held Wrack's eyes. "Come south with me, and I'll tell you all of it on the way. You'd be home in a week. You'd know your own front door."

It went into him like a hook. He had told himself for weeks that he no longer thought of the blank every hour. He found that his hands were shaking. He put them between his knees.

Jef said nothing. He was looking at the water.

"What am I to you?" Wrack said.

"An investment." The visitor smiled a little. It was not unkind. "Twenty-two years of one. The best I ever made."

"Then you'll know who tied my hands."

There was a pause. It was the only time the visitor seemed to choose his words.

"Yes," he said. "I do know that."

Wrack looked at the old man beside him, in the apron, with the hole in his elbow. He looked at the barred door. He thought of the warm-stone lying open on the bench, half done.

"I'll stay," he said.

"Of course you will. He's put you in a jersey and given you a stool, and you think it's a home." The visitor said it to Jef, without heat. "You did that to me."

"You were happy."

"I was twenty." He settled the helmet on its ring, and his voice came out of the suit flat and small. "I shan't come again."

"No," said Jef. "I don't expect I'd be in."

The grey needle went down from the landing and was gone. Out in the Well, one by one, the rays began to turn.

Nobody spoke for some time. Then Okonta said, "That Thread is older than mine."

"It's forty-nine years old. I laid it myself." Jef had not moved. "He was the fifty-ninth. The best head of the lot. He could draw anything he'd seen once. He's an islander. The rays pulled him out of a wreck when he was a boy and left him at the Hush, and he grew up sweeping it, as I swept the Lantern. I thought—" He stopped. "Well. He had hands like a pair of feet. Eight years, and he never opened a seam. I gave him the Thread in the end. I thought that if he could hear the machines, his hands might learn. He heard them very well."

"What does he do now?" Wrack asked.

"Owns things."

"He knows who I am."

"Yes."

"You could have let him say it."

"I could." Jef turned his head then. His bright dark eyes were very tired. "And it would have been true, every word, and it would have been his. You'd have had your whole life handed to you by that man, in his voice, with his price on it. I'll not have that. You'll get it another way or you'll not get it." He pushed himself up off the step, with a hand on Wrack's shoulder. "There's a thing I'd meant to leave till the spring," he said, to nobody. "I don't think I'll leave it."

The cold deepened. The sun dropped a little lower each day on its round, and the ice above the house groaned at night. Wrack had been at Kettle House two months. He could strip an air-heart and put it back. He could hear a crab walking at the bottom of the Well. Somewhere in those weeks he had stopped thinking of the blank in his head every hour, and thought of it only every day.

Jef was slower. Wrack saw it, because he was watching for it. The old man sat down more often. He let Wrack carry things. One morning he could not close his hand on a tool, and he stood looking at his fingers with a kind of detached annoyance, as at a machine that had been well maintained and had no excuse.

That night he cooked.

It was an event. He did not cook, Maelis said, more than twice a year, and the results were generally a matter for healers. He sent Wrack down to the weed garden and Okonta out for crab. He stood at the stove for two hours in his apron, swearing at it. What came to the table was a stew, red with some southern spice, and it was very good. Brask and one of the cousins had come over the ice with a cask of something made from fermented seal's milk. They stayed, and filled the room. Jef told a long scandalous story about an elder of Deep Chorus four hundred years dead. Brask laughed until he had to be thumped on the back. Even Okonta smiled into her cup. Maelis watched her husband down the length of the table. She ate almost nothing.

When the Rime men had gone out into the dark, singing, Jef pushed his bowl away and laid something on the table.

It was a tool. Wrack had seen it in his hand every day, and never out of it. It was small and worn and of no shape that he could have named, and its grip had been polished by nine hundred years of one man's fingers.

"That's for you," Jef said. "I took it out of a store at the Lantern when I was nine years old. Don't thank me. It's a thing for getting into seams, and you get into them without it, so you need it less than I did. But it's the one that'll do the job you couldn't do at Low Anthem. The lamp that was broken, not slipped. You felt there was a tool for it. That's the tool."

Wrack picked it up. It fitted his hand as though it had been waiting.

"I haven't taught you to make," Jef went on. "Tend, mend, make: that's the order, and making's the long one. We'll start tomorrow, or the day after. But there's something I want done first, while I've the hands for it." He glanced at Okonta. "Tell him what it's like. He'll not believe me."

Okonta turned her cup round on the table.

"You know what I can do with it," she said. "You've ridden behind me. What I've not told you is what it's like to have it." She touched the scar at her temple. "He lays a thread along the nerves, from here to the base of the skull. It's Maker stuff, he draws it himself. It takes a night. You sleep through it. When you wake, the old machines aren't things any more. They're more of you. I know where my needle is now, down at the landing, the way I know where my left hand is. When I'm riding, I hear through it for eighty kilometres." She paused. "And you're never by yourself again. Some of us can't bear that. There's always the hum of something, far off. It's company. I've come to like it."

"Does it hurt?"

"For a week. Like a tooth."

"You steered my Dragon's water from the outside," Jef said to Wrack, "like a man pushing a cart. It let you, because it's a patient old thing. You ought to be able to drive it. Feel it from the inside. Know what's wrong with it from a thousand kilometres off, before it stops." He rubbed his eyes. "I haven't laid a Thread in six years. There was a boy from Fallow; he was the last. It takes more out of me each time. I think I've one more."

"Do I have to swear? The rule?"

"No. I'm not making a monk of you. You've enough to carry." He almost smiled. "I'm making a mechanic who can hear his own machines. It's what I'd have given anything for at your age. I had to go round and knock on them."

Maelis had not spoken. She got up now and went into the still-room, where she kept her jars, and shut the door. After a moment Jef got up, slowly, with a hand on the table, and went in after her.

Wrack and Okonta sat and looked at the lamp. They could not hear the words. They heard her voice, low and steady and then not steady. They heard his going on, gently, for a long time. Once her voice cracked. Then there was silence. It went on so long that Okonta rose and began, very quietly, to clear the bowls.

When the door opened it was Maelis who came out. Her face was perfectly composed.

"Tomorrow night," she said to Wrack. "Eat nothing after noon. I'll give you something to make you sleep." She took her coat from its peg. "I'm going down to talk to the rays."

He spent the next day doing nothing, by order. In the afternoon Jef found him sitting on the lip of the Well.

The old man lowered himself down beside him with a grunt. They sat with their feet in the water, like two boys on a dock. The jellies went by. Far below, the star had nine points.

"She thinks it'll finish me," Jef said. "She's probably right. She generally is. I told her I'd rather be finished doing the one thing nobody else can do than last another year complaining about my knees." He threw a pebble and watched it sink, down and down, until it was gone. "She said that was a selfish thing to say to a woman. I said it was. Fifty-one years. You'd think I'd have learned to argue with her."

"Then don't do it. Teach me to make instead. I'd rather have that."

"You'd rather. Yes. So would I." He was silent a little. "Listen. I'll tell you something I've not said to an apprentice before. I never wanted to be the only one. People think I did. They think I kept it close. I'd have given it to anyone with the hands. I spent three hundred years begging holds to send me their cleverest children. They sent me their most devout ones." He shook his head. "I got to be a god because I was the last man who could mend a lamp. That's all a god is. Somebody people can't do without. It's a terrible thing to be. Don't let them do it to you. Teach everybody. Teach people you don't like."

"I will."

"You say that. You don't know yet what you'll want to keep." He looked at Wrack. It was a long, shrewd, not unkind look. "You've something under there, where your name was. I've seen you not thinking about it. I had one of those. I'll not tell you mine, and I'll not ask yours. But I'll tell you this for nothing. It doesn't get lighter for being left. I gave mine nine hundred years to get lighter."

He put his hand on Wrack's shoulder, and used it to push himself to his feet.

"Come up when the sun's at the notch," he said. "Don't be late. I'm not so sure of my hands after dark."

Wrack remembered little of the night. There was a bitter drink in a clay cup, and Maelis's cool hand on his forehead. There was the long table in the workshop, cleared for the first time, with a clean cloth on it. A cold white light hung overhead. Jef's face was above him, upside down, intent and steady. The old hands were quite sure of themselves. He heard the old man say, to nobody, "There you are. Now then." After that he heard nothing.

He woke in the box bed by the window, and the world had a second edge to it.

His head ached from the temples to the nape, as Okonta had promised. But it was the other thing he noticed. He could feel the needle moored at the landing as if it were his own foot. He could feel the deep-runner beside it, and the warm-stone on the workshop bench. Every lamp in the house was a small bright pressure, like sun on closed eyelids. Further off, there was a great slow pulse below him that was the heart of the Lantern. And a thousand kilometres south, moving, was a long bright hum that he would have known anywhere.

Jef was asleep in the chair beside the bed, with his chin on his chest and his apron still on.

"He wanted to be here when you woke," Maelis said from the door.

He did not wake that day. Wrack carried him to the box bed, and was shocked at how little he weighed. Maelis sat with him. She did not use her gift. Wrack asked her once, and she said that there was nothing wrong with him to mend. He was only very tired, she said, like a road near its end.

On the second night Wrack woke in the loft with his heart going hard.

Something was coming. He felt it with the new sense, the one that still ached. It was far to the south, under the ice, and it was fast. It was narrow and bright, like a needle. But a needle with a rider had a warmth in it; he had learned that from Okonta's. This had none. It was cold all through, and there was an edge on it.

He was down the ladder before he knew he had moved. Okonta was at the door with her hand to her temple.

"There's nobody on it," she said. "Something is driving it from a long way back. There's a hull sitting at the ice edge, eighty kilometres south. It isn't moving. It's listening." She looked at him. "Old pattern."

It went under the house. He felt it pass, like a draught under a door. Then it tipped, and went down the Well.

He ran to the lip and put his hand in the water. He felt the wardens rise. They came off the walls all together, a kilometre down, a whole forest of arms closing across the shaft. The thing went through them. He felt that too, and wished he had not.

Far below, the star flared white. Then it shrank. Two of its points went out, and then a third.

In his head the great slow pulse of the Lantern's heart staggered, and caught, and staggered again.

"That's a dart."

Jef was standing in the doorway in his nightshirt, holding on to the frame. Maelis was behind him. He was looking at nothing. He was listening, as Okonta listened.

"That's one of mine," he said. "I made forty of them, once. I broke thirty-eight with a hammer. I looked eight hundred years for the other two." He let out a breath. "Trust Anselm. He always could find a thing. He only couldn't mend it."

"There are three hundred people in those pools," Maelis said.

"I know how many there are." He had straightened. Wrack saw, with a kind of dread, that the tiredness had gone out of him. He looked as he looked at the bench when a thing was laid open on it. "Get the runner, Okonta. Wrack, you'll hold the water, as you did before. All of it. You'll start now, from here. You'll hold it the whole way down, and you'll not let go, whatever you see. Can you?"

"Yes."

"I'll want my apron."

Maelis had it in her hand already. She put it over his head and tied it behind him. Her fingers were quite steady. For a moment she stood with her forehead against the back of his neck. Neither of them said anything. Then she took down her coat.

It was nine hours to the bottom, and afterwards Wrack remembered almost none of it. He lay along the flank of the deep-runner with one arm in the water and held three hundred pools still. They were further off than they had ever been, and he was less afraid. He asked, and went on asking, and the old water of the Well leaned in and took the weight with him. Beside him, on the runner, Jef said something to Maelis, quietly, at some length. She answered him once.

They passed the wardens. Two of them were drifting loose, with their great arms trailing. The rest had drawn back against the wall. A gold eye followed the runner down.

One hall of the nine was dark. It was cracked from roof to floor, and the sea stood in it. The centre of the ring had been stove in. The dart was still there. It was sunk to half its length in the floor above the heart, a pale sliver no longer than a man, and it was humming faintly. The healers had not left their pools. They sat at every one with their hands in the water, three to a pool, in what light there was. Some of them were weeping. None of them had moved.

Jef looked at the dart for a moment. Then he got down on his knees, with a grunt, at the mouth of the crawlway.

"Lend me that," he said.

Wrack did not understand. Then he took the tool out of his pocket and put it into the old man's hand.

"I'll want it back," Jef said. "Don't come down. You've enough to do." He put the lamp between his teeth, and went in.

He was eleven hours under the floor.

Wrack could not go to him this time. The cracked hall was letting the cold in, and the pools wanted to know it. It took everything he had to keep them from knowing. He heard the old man's voice now and then, calling up for something, and a young healer ran with it. Later the voice came less often. Then it did not come.

Then the pulse steadied. He felt it in his head before the lamps showed it. The great heart under the ring drew one long clean beat, and another. The milk-white glass brightened round him, hall after hall. There were eight. The ninth stayed dark.

"Jef," he called down the crawlway.

He let the pools go, one at a time, each as gently as he had ever done anything. Then he went in on his elbows.

The old man was lying on his back under the heart, with the lamp beside him, still burning. One hand was up inside the works. It was resting on the thing it had seated. The other lay on his chest. It held the tool a little way out, handle first, as he had always handed a thing.

Wrack took it.

"He left something for me to tell you," Maelis said.

They had brought him up. It had taken a day. She sat on the step of Kettle House with the old man's hand in both of hers. The rays had risen out of the Well, hundreds of them. They hung motionless in the black water, wing to wing, as far as he could see.

"He said it on the runner, while you were busy. He said: *he has to ask for his memory himself.* Not be told it. Not by Anselm, and not by me. He said you'd understand it when it was time."

"He hadn't finished teaching me."

"No."

"I can't make. He said tomorrow. Or the day after."

"I know what he said." She laid Jef's hand down and smoothed it. "I'm taking him south. He liked the hot springs. He always said he was never once warm enough in this house."

"Who is he? The man who came."

"A boy I grew up with." Her voice was perfectly level. "Don't ask me now. When you're ready for my question, come to the Hush. I'll tell you about him then. It's the same story." She stood. She seemed taller than before, and much further away. "Not before."

The people of Rime came over the ice that day, all six hundred of them, and stood along the shelf without a word. The healers had come up with him out of the Well, in a long grey line, and they stood beside the ice-divers. Brask carried the old man down to the landing himself. He would not let anyone help.

The Dragon came round at dusk, at a walk. Maelis stepped aboard with Jef in her arms. The rays went too, all of them, flying beside it in the red twilight like an escort of sails.

Wrack stood on the landing with the tool in his fist. Okonta stood beside him. She had been south to the ice edge on her needle that morning, and had found open water with nothing in it. She said nothing now, and he was grateful for it.

Far out over the Well, the Dragon's long pale light flickered, steadied, and flickered again.
